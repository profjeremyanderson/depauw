Prompt for in-class essay #1, on Realism and/or Pacifism
Your tasks for this assignment:
  • Pick some significant thing from Thucydides' Melian Debate, or Machiavelli's The Prince, or Lackey's essay on pacifism
  • Explain the author's view on that thing, and
  • Critically evaluate their view on that thing.
That's pretty vague, so let me give you some examples. The list below isn't restrictive; you may write about other things. But these are good examples of things to write about because they (a) get at significant aspects of our readings, (b) none of them is simple (each requires a good deal of explaining to get right), (c) there's a good deal of textual evidence you can look at to support what you say, and (d) each is controversial.
  • To write about the Melian Debate, you might do one of the following:
    • Explain the Realism expressed by Athens (which we outlined in class), then critically evaluate it
    • Discuss whether Melos should have accepted Athens' offer instead of resisting. To do this, you might:
      • Explain the reasons Athens gives for surrendering, and then critically examine them
      • Explain the reasons (or just some of the main reasons) Melos gives for not surrendering, and then critically examine them
      • (Try not to use hindsight; evaluate either side's reasons from the point of view of someone at the debate.)
  • To write about The Prince, you might do one of these:
    • Explain, and then critically evaluate, Machiavelli's view on cruelty: what is it? Should we agree or not? How come? 
    • Explain, then critically evaluate, Machiavelli's view on the importance of perception
    • Same, on Machiavelli's view on being moral vs. being immoral
    • Same, re the importance Machiavelli places on thinking about and preparing for war
    • Same, re the importance Machiavelli places on avoiding hatred and contempt
    • Same, re Machiavelli's concept of virtue
    • Same, re Machiavelli on fortune
    • Same, re Machiavelli on being loved vs. feared
  • To write about Lackey's "Pacifism," you might:
    • Lackey's discussion of anti-war pacifism is long and multi-faceted, so don't take on all of it. Instead consider some chunk of it:
      • Explain why he thinks killing soldiers is problematic, and then critically evaluate that
      • Lackey discusses various ways that killing civilians might be defended, and rejects them. How does he do it? How successful is he?
      • Lackey argues that the balance of good and evil in war favors pacifism. How does he do that? How successful is he?
    • Pick one of the other forms of pacifism he explains, explain his criticism of it, and critically evaluate it. Is Lackey fair? Is there some criticism of that form of pacifism that he missed? Is there some defense of that form of pacifism that he overlooked, or should reconsider?
Which one to pick? One you believe you understand very well. Only then will your explanation be good, and good critical evaluation begins with a good understanding of what you're evaluating.

You will have the whole class period to write. You may not need the entire hour, but I recommend you take it. Essays that say more generally do better than those that say less.

Integrity: No texts, notes, devices, or other aids allowed. You must write your essay with just your brains and hands. I will provide blue books, and pens if you need to borrow one.



Academic integrity in this course
In-class essays and exams must be written without books, notes, devices, or other aids. Because you don't have access to sources, no citations or quoting are required.

Writing done outside of class, cush as reading responses, out-of-class papers if any, presentations, and any take-home exams must meet the requirements laid out below.

This section will not discuss all kinds of academic integrity. It will focus on the problems that occur most commonly. You are still responsible for following DePauw's Academic Integrity Policy.

The most common integrity problems are:

A. Plagiarism -- that is, presenting others' work as if it were your own, intentionally or not -- and
B. Crediting others' work, but badly.

To avoid both, follow the guidelines below.

A. When you include others' words or ideas in your work without making clear they aren't yours, that is plagiarism. "Others' words or ideas" includes anything you didn't make up yourself: the labor or creativity of other people, material produced by AI, etc. So, to avoid plagiarizing:
  • Any time you use something you get from another, cite it, whether you quote it or not.

    • Whatever the source was -- assigned texts, a video, class discussion, a conversation with a friend, an academic journal, a web page, your mother, space aliens, or whatever -- cite it.
  • If you use another person's words in your work, put them in quotation marks and cite.

    • FYI, if you just change some of the wording here and there you may still be plagiarizing. Either put the material in quotation marks or completely rephrase it. See this exercise for help.

  • Keep track of your sources.

    • Citing properly will be easy if you make a note of what you read and who you talk to about your work. Keep track of what words and ideas come from each source. Then, if you use any words or ideas from your sources, cite them and put their words in quotation marks.
  • Unsure what to do? Ask me before you turn in the work.

Citing and quoting properly are vital. Failing to cite and quote as required will automatically lower your grade, potentially down to failing. I may also charge you with violating DePauw’s Academic Integrity Policy. If the charge is upheld, the minimum penalty is worse than turning in no work at all. You must keep track of, and properly document, whose words and ideas you are using. DePauw provides lots of info on understanding academic integrity and how to avoid plagiarism on this helpful page.


B. How to cite various sources
  • Readings with page numbers: Give the author's name and the page number in parentheses, like this: (Machiavelli, 58) or (Kilner, 30).
  • Readings with paragraph numbers: give the author's name and the paragraph number.
  • Readings without page or paragraph numbers: If the assigned text is, for example, a web page with no page numbers, just cite the author, like this: (Gladwell). If it lacks page numbers but is divided into sections of some sort, give the author and section -- for example, an online version of Machiavelli's The Prince will have chapter numbers, so give them: (Machiavelli, ch. 18).

  • Videos/films: Give the video/film's title and the time at which the cited material begins, like this: (Munich, 1:04:30) or (2022-02-11 Phil 309 recording, 25:08).

  • Citing class discussion or lecture:
    • Note: please don't rely only on class notes for material that is in assigned texts or videos, because the original source -- the text or video -- is usually a richer source. Also, since we're fallible, notes may be inaccurate. Use the notes to help yourself understand the original source and, if you use words or ideas from notes, you must quote and/or cite them. But, as much as you can, work from the original source and quote/cite it.
    • Class notes: cite the class and date, either like (Phil 309, 2022-02-09) or (2022-02-09 Phil 309 notes)
    • Class discussion: same as for class notes.
    • Recorded class meeting (for classes taught remotely): same as above for videos/films.

Works Cited/Bibliography/References:  When you use any sources outside of class materials, include a Works Cited for them. (So, obviously you'll need them for your presentation.) If you only use class materials (which is OK for short papers), a Works Cited is optional in this course.

You may use any standard citation format. I happen to like Chicago and  Turabian, and the Library website has guides to different formats including  a quick guide to Turabian here; click the  Turabian (P/RL)  tab for instructions on citing various sources.


Guidelines for papers written outside of class
Your papers should be primarily argumentative. That is, they should defend a conclusion which other smart people might well disagree with, and this must be your paper's major focus. If you'd like to do something else, come discuss it with me.

Since other smart people might well disagree with you, you should seriously consider some objection(s) to your stance.

Choose your sources wisely when doing any research. Use my advice here in the Presentation assignment.

Format, style, and such

Start your paper with a short introduction including a thesis and road map. By "short" I mean no more than half a page in a short paper, no more than a page in a long paper. In the intro, clearly state what overall point you will argue for (your thesis) and briefly explain the main steps you'll take in the paper to argue for that point (your road map). The thesis and road map are the most important things in your introduction.

Submit your paper as a Word (.docx or .doc) or Rich Text Format (.rtf) file, or share a Google doc. Please no PDF's or Pages files.

Put your name at the top. Double space, one-inch margins, numbered pages, 12-point Times New Roman or similar font.

As far as style goes, I am more impressed by writers who express ideas clearly using simple language than by those who use big words and long sentences. It is better to write like Michael Moore (see "The Argument for Retributivism") and less like John Stuart Mill. (I only commend Moore's style, not his logic.) You may write in first person if you like.

Proofread carefully. Do not trust your college GPA to your computer's grammar- and spell-checkers.



 What I'm looking for in your short papers done outside of class
Each of your short papers should be about 1200-1500 words long (roughly 4-5 pages). In each, take a position regarding some of the material we have studied and defend it against some objection(s).

Since the paper is short, you will need to limit its scope. Take on a small question rather than a large one. For example, do not try to show that natural law is superior to legal positivism, or decide whether the insanity defense should be eliminated. (These would be tough to do even in your research paper.) It may also be too much to take on the argument of an entire text. For example, Kadish's article "The Criminal Law and the Luck of the Draw" has three main theses and considers failed attempts, culpable risk creation, impossible attempts, the purposes of the criminal law...lots of big issues, with multiple arguments concerning several of them. That is too much to take on in a short paper. Instead, choose some particular argument in a text. For example, is Kadish right to reject a retributive justification for the harm doctrine? Or, is he right that the harm doctrine fails to serve the purpose of the criminal law? Or you might set aside the question of whether an author's conclusion is correct and focus more on the quality of an argument, critically evaluating it. Or you might write about a legal ruling such as People v. Dlugash. Rulings often contain multiple arguments, any one of which may be worth critically evaluating.

Pick a topic that interests you. One place to look for potential topics is in your own reading responses: what portion of the readings has most intrigued you? Is there a particular argument or issue you want to write about? Feel free to discuss possible topics with me.

Follow the guidelines for all papers. No outlines or drafts are required, though discussing your paper with me or turning in a draft well before the due date would be helpful, as would discussing your paper with folks at the Writing Center.

Obviously your argument regarding one assigned text may be helped, or opposed, by points made by the author of another text. In such cases, by all means bring in other authors' arguments.

No research is required, though it can enhance the paper. If you use any sources outside of assigned texts and your own thinking, then heed the advice on research.

Due dates will be on the class schedule Google doc.

 

 Some possible paper strategies
These are just suggestions; your paper need not do any of them. Still, they may be helpful:
  • Show that position X seems plausible but really suffers from significant objection O. (For example: "So-and -so believes that threatening to use nuclear weapons is never justified, and gives appealing reasons for this position. But I'll show that, at least under certain circumstances, it is justified.")
  • Show that position X seems to suffer from objection O, but in fact it does not.
  • Discuss so-and-so's justification for position X, showing that X may be correct but the justification is faulty. (For example, you might like Realism but dispute a particular argument for it, so show that although that argument may be popular or influential it's really not good--and/or supply a better one.)
  • Discuss so-and-so's justification for position X, showing that although the justification may seem bad it's really not.
  • Show that P is a vexing problem, for which S is a more promising solution than some alternative(s). (For example: "Here are some proposed responses to al Qaeda-type terrorism; of them, S is superior.")
  • Show that P is a vexing problem for which S seems like a promising solution, but really S is bad (and, perhaps, that we should do T instead).
